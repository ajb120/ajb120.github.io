Cheyenne Mountain Zoo Website Redesign

This project was a semester-long redesign of the Cheyenne Mountain Zoo's website, focused on the homepage and ticket purchasing flow. The current site makes it difficult for visitors to find and buy tickets, creating a frustrating experience before they even arrive at the zoo. The outcome was a clickable, high-fidelity prototype with a streamlined ticket purchase flow.

User Experience Design 1
01

Introduction

I was the sole designer and researcher responsible for all stages of this project, from initial usability testing through final prototype delivery.

The core problem was making the ticket buying process easier and more intuitive for zoo visitors. My limitations included a one-semester timeline, a small testing pool, working solo without a team to collaborate with, and designing for a real brand without access to their official assets or guidelines.

02

Research

To evaluate the current Cheyenne Mountain Zoo website, I recruited 5 participants between the ages of 18–64 who had previously visited a zoo and made an online purchase. I observed each participant as they navigated the site and attempted to purchase tickets, taking notes on where they struggled and what confused them. The biggest pain point was the number of steps required to actually get to the ticket purchasing page. Users had to click through multiple pages and agree to terms before even seeing ticket options. Once they reached the ticket selection page, users found it visually overwhelming and dense with text. Overall, participants felt the process was functional but far more complicated than it needed to be.

How might we statement: How might we streamline the ticket purchasing process so that users can complete their purchase quickly and confidently?

03

Design

Problem statement

Visitors to the Cheyenne Mountain Zoo website need a clearer and more streamlined way to purchase tickets in order to complete their purchase quickly and confidently without confusion or unnecessary effort.

Task flow

I mapped out the full ticket purchasing journey to identify every step a user would need to take from the homepage to order confirmation. The task flow covers seven steps: homepage, ticket landing page, visit details, add to cart, billing and shipping details, payment, and order confirmation. This helped me identify where the current site added unnecessary friction and where I could consolidate steps in my redesign.

Low fidelity wireframes

I started with low fidelity wireframes to establish the basic layout and structure of each screen before committing to any visual design decisions. This stage was about getting the skeleton of the flow right. I figured out where elements would live on the page and how a user would move through each step. I focused on the same core screens that follow the task flow, from the homepage through to order confirmation.

Mid fidelity wireframes

Building on the low fidelity layouts, I created mid fidelity wireframes to add more detail and begin refining the structure of each screen. This included placeholder content, more defined UI elements like buttons and form fields, and clearer spacing between sections. The mid fidelity stage helped me think through the default and filled-in states of the ticket purchasing flow before moving into high fidelity.

High fidelity interactive prototype

The high fidelity prototype brought the redesign to life with full visual design, real content, and clickable interactions across the entire ticket purchasing flow. I applied consistent typography, color, and spacing throughout and designed both default and filled-in states for every screen so the prototype could be tested realistically.

04

Evaluation & Results

User testing process and pain points

I conducted usability testing with 3 participants on my high fidelity v1 prototype, walking each person through the full ticket purchasing flow and observing where they ran into confusion. Several consistent pain points came up across all three sessions. Every participant was confused by the cart displaying a $0 total after adding tickets, assuming the site had malfunctioned. All three were also frustrated by the absence of a date and time selection step in the flow, leaving them unsure whether the zoo would know when they were visiting. Finally, multiple participants struggled to locate the primary action buttons like Submit and Pay, as they didn't stand out enough from the rest of the page. Instructor feedback

Throughout the design process, my instructor left feedback directly in my Figma file. The main notes centered on making sure all elements were properly snapped to the grid and ensuring each screen had no more than one primary action button at a time. I incorporated both of these into my final prototype iteration, editing each screen for grid alignment and adjusting any pages where multiple primary buttons were competing for attention.

Updates

Based on user feedback, I made several targeted updates to the prototype. I fixed the cart flow in Figma so users can clearly see their ticket total update as they add tickets, giving them confidence their selection is registered correctly. I also added a date and time selection step earlier in the purchasing flow so users always know their visit details are captured before reaching checkout. Primary action buttons were redesigned to be more visually prominent throughout the flow.

05

Reflection

Future plans

With more time I would expand the redesign beyond the ticket purchasing flow to include other pages of the Cheyenne Mountain Zoo website, like the animals or experiences pages, to create a more complete and cohesive redesign. Additional rounds of user testing would help validate the changes made between iterations and surface any remaining pain points. I would also explore A/B testing different color schemes and typography to see which combinations users respond to best, since there wasn't enough time this semester to experiment with those decisions.

Conclusion

This project taught me a lot about what it actually takes to design a thoughtful digital experience. I got much more comfortable working with grid systems and spacing in increments of 8, which made my layouts feel more polished and intentional. I also developed a stronger eye for color and brand consistency — learning how to make design decisions that feel cohesive across an entire flow rather than screen by screen. Most importantly, going through the full cycle of wireframing, user testing, incorporating feedback, and iterating gave me a real understanding of the UX design process from start to finish. It's one thing to learn these steps in theory and another to actually apply them to a real website with real users giving you feedback.
